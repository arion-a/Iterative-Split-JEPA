Iterative SplitJEPA: what has been done

The project asks one question: if a learned representation can be split once into a part that stays the same across matched examples (invariant) and a part that changes (variant), can the invariant part be split again, and again, to recover a ladder of nested hidden layers of the world? This page states exactly what was built, what it showed, and which part of the goal each piece served.

Short answer. The mechanism is validated in controlled worlds where the layers are planted and exact matched pairs are given: it recovers each layer to about 1 degree, to five layers deep. It has not been shown to produce useful invariants on real data, and it has not been shown to pay off downstream. A fix for the last open encoder problem (the slowest layer being lost at depth) is running now (E45, see section 8).

1. The idea in one picture

The representation of the world is a vector. The method peels it like layers: rung 1 removes the fastest-changing block, rung 2 removes the next, and so on. What is left after the last rung is the slowest block, the content that stays constant across everything the matched pairs vary.

3. What one rung looks like: a gap means a block exists

4. The one clean discovery: correspondence, not speed, carries the split (E38)

If layers change at different speeds, a single eigendecomposition of adjacent-frame differences can order them by speed and look like it works. If two layers change at the same speed, that eigenspace is degenerate: any rotation inside it is equally valid, so the found axis is arbitrary. Pairs from a correspondence family expose exactly one layer whatever its speed, so the ladder is unaffected.

6. Why a generic encoder failed, and what was found

A generic MLP trained only to predict drops the fast layer. Adding the invariance term made it worse in a way that looked like the theory failing but was an execution problem: the encoder's outputs had collapsed to fewer directions than the world has dimensions, so the layer had nowhere to go.

8. Where the project is now

Running: E45. E44 showed the generic encoder keeps the rungs clean to depth 5 but loses the slowest block (presence 0.56 and 0.33) and has dead output directions. The cause found: the whitening penalty was divided by the number of outputs, so a dead direction cost 1/d of its weight, too little at 16 and 20 outputs. The fix uses plain sums so a dead direction costs the same at any width; the weight is chosen on a pilot by a no-dead-direction rule, and the 4- and 5-block runs are repeated on the same ten seeds. Pass lines, registered first: every rung clean on at least 7 of 10 seeds, no dead direction on at least 9 of 10, and the slowest block's presence at least 0.95 on at least 7 of 10. Result: pending (to be added here).

What is not achieved

  • No result on real or image data with a trained encoder; built pairs beyond rung 2 were never tested.
  • No demonstrated payoff of the hierarchy.
  • The one distinguishing positive result (E38) is in worlds we designed.

What would decide the open questions

  • The robot head trained with the whitening terms, scored on held-out scenes against a plain contrastive baseline.
  • Rung-3 pair building in the mixture world, to see how errors compound.
  • One confirmatory run of the E43 recipe on fresh seeds, registered first.
