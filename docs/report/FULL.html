Iterated splitting: method, results, assumptions

What was done, on what data, what came out, and what it rests on. Plain language; every number is from the runs.

1. The question

A video frame becomes a point in a space with many coordinates. A frame is built from layers of hidden numbers that change at different speeds (some change every frame, some almost never). Can we split the space, layer by layer, from the fastest layer to the slowest, using only pairs of points?

2. The data and the points

Hidden numbers (generated by me)

Each sample has several layers; one layer is a group of 4 numbers. At each time step every number is updated by

new = keep × old + √(1 − keep²) × fresh random number

keep is the layer's speed setting: 0.2 means mostly fresh each frame (fast), 0.95 means almost unchanged (slow). The √ part keeps the overall spread the same. The speeds used run from 0.2 up to 0.95.

From hidden numbers to points

A fixed bend mixes the numbers, standing in for "a camera turns a scene into pixels". The numbers are held as a left half and a right half, and four steps each add a bent copy of one half to the other:

right = right + tanh( left × W₁ )    left = left + tanh( right × W₂ )
right = right + tanh( left × W₃ )    left = left + tanh( right × W₄ )

Here W₁…W₄ are fixed tables of numbers whose size is set by the bend strength, and tanh squashes a value smoothly into −1 … 1. Then an encoder turns the result into the final point (in the simplest runs it is left as is; in others it is trained so two neighbouring frames land close together without the points collapsing). Finally the points are whitened:

point = C−1/2 × ( raw point − average )    where C = how the raw points spread (their covariance)

Whitening only removes stretching: afterwards the cloud of points is equally spread in every direction. The bend is still there.

3. The method

One rung asks about one layer, from fastest to slowest:

  1. Pair. Take two samples that agree on every layer slower than the one asked about and differ in it. (Here I copy those layers from the generator, so the pairs are exact.)
  2. Subtract. For every pair: D = point₁ − point₂. Only the asked layer (and faster ones, already removed) make D non-zero.
  3. Measure the spread of the differences. M = average of D × Dᵀ is a table of how the differences spread (a covariance table), computed only inside the directions not yet removed.
  4. Find its long axes (eigendecomposition): M = Σ λᵢ vᵢ vᵢᵀ. The 4 directions with the largest λ are the block for the layer. The rest are what did not change. gap = λ(smallest big) ÷ λ(largest small): big means a clean cut, 1 means no cut.
  5. Repeat inside the directions that did not change, with pairs for the next layer.

Seven layers of 4 numbers: 28 directions. Each rung removes one block; the last 4 directions are the slowest layer.

Why it works, in one line. If a pair differs only in one layer, its difference lies along that layer's directions, so the spread table has big values there and about nothing elsewhere. Whitening makes the layers' directions perpendicular, so the long axes read off exactly that layer. Checked by hand on a 3-number example: pencil and code give the same answers.

Scoring (tests only). The hidden numbers are used only to score: the angle between the found block and the true layer's directions (0° perfect, about 80° for unrelated directions) and the gap above.

Every one of the 6 rungs finds its layer: angles 0.3 – 2.2° and gaps above 70. The error grows slowly with depth (0.4° at rung 1, about 2° at rung 6) but stays small. With a trained encoder on 4 and 5 layers (20 runs) the numbers are the same: 0.2 – 1.9°.

5.2 A stronger bend

With no encoder training, the cut fails from strength 1 upward. Training the encoder repairs strength 1 (5 of 5 runs good) but, with the standard setting, not 2, 4 or 8. A safety rule skips a rung whose gap is 10 or less; the skipped layer then stays in and the next rungs fail too. With that rule off (green), strengths 2 and 4 give partial results: angles 3 – 15° and gaps 2 – 6 (visible, not sharp). Strength 8 with the rule off was not run.

5.3 Pairs, not speed, define the layers

With all layers changing at the same speed, iterated splitting still finds each layer (under 1.1°). A single split that only looks at adjacent-frame differences cannot (74–88°). The pairs, not the speeds, decide which layer is found.

5.4 Pairs built from the data instead of copied

Without the generator, pairs can be made by grouping points into clusters and pairing points inside a cluster. This works at rung 2 (about 17°) and fades by rungs 4–5 (48°, 57°). Even perfect clusters and exact pairs lose accuracy with depth in this cluster world, because same-cluster pairs still differ a little in the slower layers. Grouping all the points, instead of only the slow part, never works. Best case: the true number of clusters was given.

5.5 Simulated robot images: the paper's method, and splitting on top of it

Data: the paper's two tasks (PickCube, PushCube), 800 trajectories each in 100 groups of 8, two cameras, 30 frames per trajectory (48,000 images per task). The score is how much farther apart a pair of views of the same frame (base camera vs side camera) land in the "variant" half than in the "invariant" half.

  • Grey – my earlier test: frozen image features and a small trained head, then iterated splitting. Blocks barely separate (about 1.1 to 2).
  • Blue – the paper's own training code on this data (a ResNet-50 fine-tuned so the first half of its output ignores the camera). The halves separate strongly: 9.3 and 12.1, close to the paper's 8.4 and 14.0. So the paper's method is reproduced here, even with shorter training.
  • Red – iterated splitting applied to that trained embedding, with the paper's block sizes: only 3.3 and 2.9, and the cut has no gap (1.0 – 1.2 at both rungs), although the camera can be read perfectly from the found block. So splitting on top of the paper-trained embedding does worse than the paper's own split.

One possible reason, not tested: the trained embedding is far from whitened (its covariance penalty is still large), so whitening before the eigen-split mixes the two halves that the training had kept apart. Conditions: 30 epochs instead of the paper's 200, one run, and a stand-in for the robot's actions (the end-effector movement between frames) because our data has none.

6. Assumptions

  1. The pairs are given and correct. The generator copies the layers that must agree. Nothing here finds pairs on its own except the cluster test in 5.4.
  2. The layers are independent, each with unit spread; the hidden numbers are Gaussian (clustered in 5.4).
  3. Layer sizes and order are known: each layer has 4 numbers and layers are taken fastest to slowest.
  4. The bend keeps all the information (it can be undone) and is mild. A strong bend smears a layer outside its own directions, which breaks the cut.
  5. Each layer's change lives in its own directions (roughly a straight-line subspace). This is the step that fails at strong bend.
  6. Enough data: 30,000 samples to whiten and 30,000 pairs per rung.
  7. Everything is synthetic, generated by me, except one simulated-robot test. The hidden numbers are used only to score.

7. What is not shown

  • Anything on real images or video (the one image test failed).
  • A benefit for prediction from having the layers.
  • A comparison with the paper's robot training method (an encoder fine-tuned so a fixed slice of its output stays the same across pairs): not run. For the synthetic world, the paper's own code already does the same whitening and eigen-split as here, once; this report repeats it layer by layer.
  • Pairs built from data beyond about rung 2 or 3, or a clean cut under a strong bend.
