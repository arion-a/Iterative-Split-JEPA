Iterated splitting: the components

Every part used in the experiments, the critical "trunk" each one stands on, the equations, and a picture wherever a step is not obvious. Tags show where a part comes from: paper the SplitJEPA paper's own method or code, ours our iterated-splitting mechanism, new added in the latest runs, setup data or tooling.

The whole thing in one paragraph. A world produces points from hidden layers that change at different speeds (A). Matched pairs that agree on all slower layers tell us which layer to look at (B). The splitting engine takes the pair differences, finds the few directions where they spread, and cuts those off as that layer's block; then it repeats inside what is left (C). An encoder turns raw images into the points when the data are pictures (D). The hidden numbers are used only to score the result (E).

A. The worlds that make the data

A1. Layers of hidden numbers setup

A sample is several layers of 4 numbers. Each number follows a first-order random walk with its own speed:

z←ρ·z+1−ρ2·ε
Trunk: the factor √(1−ρ²) keeps every number at spread 1 whatever its speed, so layers differ only in how fast they change, never in size. Small ρ = fast layer, ρ near 1 = slow layer.
{{F_SPEED}}

A2. The bend: turning hidden numbers into points setup

Writing the numbers as a left half L and a right half R, four alternating steps add a bent copy of one half to the other:

R←R+tanh(LW1)L←L+tanh(RW2)then the same withW3,W4
Trunk: each step only adds something to one half, so it can be undone exactly: no information is lost, the layers are bent but never merged. The size of the W tables is the bend strength (0.5 = mild; the bend sweep goes up to 4).
{{F_BEND}}

A square grid of hidden numbers (2 numbers, strength 1.3 for visibility) and the same grid after the four steps.

A3. Whitening ours

r=C−1/2(e−μ)C=1N∑i(ei−μ)(ei−μ)T
Trunk: after whitening the cloud is equally spread in every direction, so a direction can only stand out in the pair differences because a layer changed there, not because the data happened to be stretched that way. (On robot embeddings, whitening can mix halves that were already separated; the later runs also cut without it.)
{{F_WHITE}}

A4. Robot data: groups, trajectories, frames, cameras setup

ManiSkill3 (PickCube and PushCube). One group is one layout (same cube and goal) run 8 times with perturbed motions; each run is a trajectory of 30 frames seen by 2 cameras: 100 groups × 8 × 30 × 2 = 48,000 images per task. The slowest thing is the layout, then which trajectory, then progress along it, and the camera is the fastest nuisance. Training uses 6 of the 8 trajectories per group; scoring uses the other 2.

Trunk: the nesting is layout > trajectory > progress > camera. The data supply matched pairs for exactly these three splits, which is why the robot ladder stops at rung 3.

A5. Re-rendered scene factors new

One PickCube scene is rendered again and again with nine nested factors set directly in the simulator (fast to slow): gripper opening (1 number), arm pose (2), cube yaw (1), cube position (2), cube colour (3), light tint (2), brightness (1), camera distance and elevation (2), camera azimuth (1). 96 × 96 images, one camera.

Trunk: the simulator lets us change one named factor and nothing else, so the pairs are exact and every recovered block can be named.

B. Matched pairs: how a layer is chosen

B1. The pair rule ours

For rung k, take a sample u and make v by keeping every layer slower than k and drawing fresh values for layers 1 … k:

vj={uj  if j>k (slower layers agree)a fresh draw  if j≤k (layers already cut, and the layer asked about)
Trunk: the pair is what defines a layer. The speeds play no role: with every layer at the same speed the pairs still pick them out (this is what a method that only compares adjacent frames cannot do).

B3. Pairs built from the data instead of supplied ours

When no generator supplies the pairs, points are grouped by k-means and two points inside the same cluster are paired (each point used once). The clusters stand in for "agrees on the slower layers".

Trunk: the quality of the clustering is the ceiling. Clusters mix points that differ a little in the slower layers, so the error grows with depth even though the splitting itself is the same.

C. The splitting engine ours

C1. Pair difference and its spread

For each pair, D = r(u) − r(v) (whitened points). The spread of the differences is the matrix

M=1N∑iDiDiT

Why it isolates a layer. In the ideal case whitening turns the hidden numbers into an orthogonal mixture Q of themselves, and the difference of two independent unit-spread numbers has spread 2. So a pair for rung k gives

D=Q(δ1,…,δk,0,…,0)M=Q·diag(2,…,2,0,…,0)QT
Trunk: eigenvalues are 2 where the pair differs and 0 where it agrees. That clean two-level pattern is what the splitting reads; the bend and finite samples only blur it.
{{F_DIFF}}

C2. Eigen-split: the closed-form answer to the paper's invariance problem

M=∑jλjvjvjTvariant block V=[v1…vd]left-over H=[vd+1…]

Asking for the directions Q that change least across pairs is the paper's invariance objective:

minQTQ=I1N∑i‖QTDi‖2=∑j∈smallestλj
Trunk: that minimum has an exact solution, the eigenvectors of M with the smallest eigenvalues. The paper's synthetic code solves it once this way; its robot code approximates it by gradient descent during training. Here the exact solution is used, rung after rung.

C3. How big is the block, and how sharp is the cut

gap=λdλd+1d=largest d with λd−λd+1≥0.1·λ1
Trunk: the gap is the ladder's own confidence meter, with no hidden numbers needed: near 1 means no cut exists; large means the layer sits in a clean block. The synthetic runs give d = 4 directly; robot and scene runs use the absolute-gap rule (right-hand formula, with the largest-ratio fallback flagged).
{{F_EIG}}

Real eigenvalues from the 6-rung world: the 4 directions of the layer stand far above the rest, at rung 1 and again at rung 6.

C4. Nesting and the ladder

Rung k works inside the directions left by rung k−1, written as a basis Hk−1. The spread is measured there, and the answer is mapped back:

Mk=Hk−1TMHk−1Mk=UΛUTVk=Hk−1U[:,:d]Hk=Hk−1U[:,d:]
Trunk: because each block is taken inside the previous left-over, blocks cannot overlap and the dimensions simply add up: each rung removes d directions, and the last left-over is the slowest layer.
{{F_LADDER}}

D. Encoders: from raw data to the points

D1. No encoder (synthetic worlds) ours

The bent points are simply whitened (A3). This isolates the splitting: any error is the engine's or the bend's.

D2. A trained encoder with the paper's objective paper

For 4- and 5-layer synthetic tests an invertible coupling encoder is trained with the paper's own synthetic objective: pull the embeddings of neighbouring frames together, and a regulariser (SIGReg) keeps the embedding spread like a standard Gaussian so it cannot collapse. The trained embedding is then whitened and split exactly as above.

Trunk: the encoder must keep the information (an invertible one cannot lose it) and the objective must stop collapse; the splitting needs nothing more.

D3. The paper's robot encoder paper

A ResNet-50 (ImageNet start) with an invariant head (first dH numbers) and a variant head, trained 200 epochs (batch 32, learning rate 3e-4) with four loss terms:

L=Lpred+Linv+Lvar+0.04·Lcov
Lvar=∑jmax(0,1−std(hj))Lcov=1d∑i≠jCij2

Linv makes the invariant half agree for the same frame seen by the two cameras; Lpred predicts the next frame's embedding from this frame and the action; the last two stop collapse (every number spread out, numbers uncorrelated). Term names are as printed in the training log; the predictor's internal details are the paper's code, which ran unchanged.

Trunk: the invariance term is the same quantity as the minimization in C2; training solves it by gradient descent, the eigen-split solves it in closed form on the finished embedding. This is why the two can be compared directly.

D4. The scene-factor autoencoder new

L=1N∑i‖xi−dec(enc(xi))‖2

Trained for 25 minutes on CPU, no labels, no pairs; 48 numbers per image. Trunk: an autoencoder is forced to keep whatever it needs to redraw the picture, so factors that are large in the image survive; very small ones (gripper, arm pose, cube yaw) do not, which is where the splitting finds no cut.

E. Scoring: how a result is judged

E1. Angle to the true block ours

The hidden numbers are used only here. A ridge fit maps hidden numbers to the whitened points; the columns for one layer span the "true block". The largest principal angle θ between the found block V and the true block T:

cosθi=σi(QVTQT)

(Q = an orthonormal basis, σ = singular values.) 0° is perfect; unrelated directions sit near 80°.

{{F_ANGLE}}

E2. Readouts and the reading matrix new

When the layers are named factors, how well a factor can be read from a block is the held-out ridge R²:

R2=1−∑(y−y^)2∑(y−y¯)2

Rows = rungs, columns = factors: a good ladder is a diagonal. A factor counts as retained when the whole embedding reads it at R² ≥ 0.5; a factor the encoder dropped cannot be expected from any block.

{{F_READ}}

E3. The camera ratio (robot) paper

For pairs of views of the same frame, how far apart they land in a block, divided by the same for random frames, then variant over invariant:

resp(B)=mean ‖(u−v)B‖22·rms spread of the points in Bratio=resp(variant half)resp(invariant half)
Trunk: the same ratio scores the paper's fixed halves and our eigen-split blocks, on the same held-out trajectories, so they are directly comparable.

E4. Controls ours

  • Shuffled pairs: keep the pair rows but permute the partners. The gap must fall to about 1 (it does, 1.0 – 1.2).
  • Random block of the same size: a block of random directions reads a factor at the level chance alone allows; a found block must beat it.
