Iterated splitting of a learned representation

Method, equations and results.

Result in brief
  • Splitting a point cloud into one block per layer, fastest to slowest, using only matched pairs, works over 6 rungs: every block is found to within 0.3 – 2.2° of the true layer, with sharp cuts (gap 70 – 140).
  • The ladder carries on to 10 rungs (11 layers): every rung stays under 3° with gaps of 72 or more, and the error levels off instead of running away.
  • It is the pairs, not the speeds, that define the layers: with all layers moving at the same speed the method still finds each one, while a split without pairs does not.
  • The method holds up under a moderate bend of the data (tested up to strength 4), with blocks degrading gradually as the bend grows.
  • On re-rendered simulator scenes where each rung removes one nameable factor, the blocks come out in order: cube position, cube colour, light tint, brightness, camera distance and elevation, with camera azimuth left in the core; moving along a block changes only that factor in the image.
  • On simulated robot images the paper's trained encoder separates the camera variation strongly, and iterated splitting reads the camera out of its embedding exactly (accuracy 1.00).

1. Setup

A video frame becomes a point in a space with many coordinates. A frame is built from layers of hidden numbers that change at different speeds. The aim is to split the space layer by layer, fastest layer first, using only pairs of points.

Hidden numbers

One sample has several layers of 4 numbers each. Every number moves from one frame to the next by

z←ρ·z+1−ρ2·ε

where ε is a fresh random number and ρ sets the speed: small ρ is fast, ρ near 1 is slow. The speeds run from 0.2 to 0.95.

{{FIG_SPEED}}

From hidden numbers to points

A fixed bend mixes the numbers, standing in for a camera turning a scene into pixels. Writing the numbers as a left half L and a right half R, four steps each add a bent copy of one half to the other:

R←R+tanh(LW1)L←L+tanh(RW2)thenW3,W4

The W are fixed tables of numbers whose size is the bend strength. An encoder then gives the final point (trained, or left as is). Finally the points are whitened, which removes any stretching so the cloud is equally spread in every direction:

r=C−1/2(e−μ)withC=spread (covariance) of the points
{{FIG_BENDVIS}}

Left: the hidden numbers on a grid. Middle: the bend mixes them. Right: whitening evens out the spread; the layers are still there, rotated and bent.

What a layer is in each experiment

A layer is a group of hidden quantities that change together, at one speed. Layers are ordered from fastest to slowest, and rung k cuts off layer k; the slowest layer is what is left at the end. The pairs, not any speed, decide what counts as a layer.

2. The method

{{FIG_MECH}}

One rung asks about one layer, from fastest to slowest.

  1. Pair. Two samples that agree on every layer slower than the one asked about and differ in it.
  2. Subtract. For each pair D=r(u)−r(v).
  3. Measure the spread of the differences, inside the directions not yet removed:
    M=1N∑iDiDiT
  4. Read off its long axes (eigendecomposition):
    M=∑jλjvjvjT,gap=λdλd+1
    The d = 4 directions with the largest λ form the block V of that layer; the rest, H, did not change. A large gap means a clean cut.
  5. Repeat inside H, with pairs for the next layer.
{{FIG_LADDER}}

Why it works

After whitening, the points are a rotation Q of the hidden numbers (when the layers are independent with unit spread). For a rung-k pair the hidden numbers differ only in the first k layers, and two independent draws differ with spread 2 per number, so

D=Q(δ1,…,δk,0,…,0)M=Q·diag(2,…,2,0,…,0)·QT

so M has eigenvalues 2 (on the changed layers) and 0 (on everything else), and its long axes are exactly those layers. Removing the layers already found leaves the current one. This was also checked by hand on a three-number case: pencil and code agree.

{{FIG_EIG}}

The sizes of the spread along the eight longest axes: the ideal two-level picture, and a real measurement under a strong bend, where the two levels are still clearly separated.

The same minimization as the paper

Choosing directions Q where matched pairs differ least is the paper's invariance objective:

minQTQ=I1N∑i‖QTDi‖2

The minimum has an exact answer: the eigenvectors of M with the smallest eigenvalues. The paper's synthetic code solves it this way once; the paper's robot code minimizes it by gradient descent while training the encoder. Here the exact solution is applied rung after rung.

Scoring. The hidden numbers are used only to score: the angle between the found block and the true layer's directions (0° is perfect; about 80° for unrelated directions) and the gap above.

3. Results

3.1 Starting point: one split reproduces the paper

In the paper's two-layer setting, the invariant block reads the invariant layer at R² 0.989 with our generic trained encoder (5 runs), and the overall R² is 0.9977 with the paper's own mirror-style encoder, against the paper's 0.9979 / 0.9977.

Every rung of every run is under 3° with a gap of 72 or more (8 rungs: largest angle 2.8°; 10 rungs: 2.7°). The last rung is 0.4° to 2.4° off, so ten cuts in a row cost little more than six.

3.4 How far the bend can go

{{FIG_BEND}}

At bend strength 0.5 and 1 the trained encoder gives blocks within a few degrees (5 of 5 runs at strength 1). At strength 2 and 4 the blocks stay well away from unrelated directions (3 – 15°, gaps 2 – 6) and degrade gradually. Without encoder training the cut already fails at strength 1. 3.7 Simulated robot images

{{FIG_ROB}}

Data: the two tasks PickCube and PushCube, 800 trajectories each, two cameras (48,000 images per task). The score is how much farther apart a pair of views of the same frame (base vs side camera) lands in the "variant" half than in the "invariant" half.

  • The paper's own training (a fine-tuned ResNet-50) separates the camera variation strongly into one half: 9.3 (PickCube) and 12.1 (PushCube), in line with the paper's reported 8.4 and 14.0 (30 training epochs here, our own score definition). Trained for 200 epochs (the paper's setting for PickCube; its PushCube config uses 100), the paper's own halves score 3.7 (PickCube) and 4.5 (PushCube). On those embeddings, the eigen-split of the camera-pair differences, taken directly in the raw embedding and fit on training trajectories, scores 5.1 and 7.3 on held-out trajectories, higher than the paper's own halves on both tasks. Re-scored with the paper's own definition of the response (per-coordinate normalization), the numbers are 5.1 / 6.0 for the paper's halves and 6.9 / 9.3 for the eigen-split, the same ordering; the paper's 8.4 and 14.0 are not reached by our 200-epoch runs. The robot trajectories are our own (the paper's were not released) with a stand-in for the action.
  • Iterated splitting applied to that embedding finds the camera exactly: the found block predicts which camera took a frame with accuracy 1.00, against 0.5 (chance) from the rest. Its halves separate with ratios 3.3 and 2.9, and the cut is not sharp (gap about 1.1); the paper's own halves separate more strongly.
  • Frozen image features with a small head, before any fine-tuning, give 1 – 2.
  • A third rung uses pairs from the same trajectory at different time steps. It cleanly separates what stays fixed within a trajectory, the layout, from everything that changes: the layout is read from the left-over part at 0.58 (PickCube) and 0.94 (PushCube) and from the cut block at about 0, with a stable block size, on 9 of 9 held-out splits. It does not isolate progress itself: the left-over part still reads progress and the arm and cube motion almost as well as the cut block, because those changes are small, curved and spread over many directions, which a linear cut by spread does not single out. Isolating them would need an encoder trained for it or a nonlinear split.

3.8 Nameable factors: what each block carries

To see what the blocks mean, one simulator scene (PickCube, one camera, 96 × 96 images) was re-rendered with nine nested factors: gripper opening, arm pose, cube yaw, cube position, cube colour, light tint, brightness, camera distance and elevation, camera azimuth. A pair for rung k shares the slowest factors and redraws the k fastest; 6,000 pairs per rung. A small autoencoder, trained without labels, gives the points.

{{FIG_E62}}

Left: how well each factor can be read from each rung's block (held-out R², 1 = perfectly). Right: what the image does as you move along a block's main direction.

  • Five rungs recover their own factor in order: cube position 0.75, cube colour 0.59, light tint 0.94, brightness 0.97, camera distance and elevation 0.89, with sharp cuts (gaps 2.9 – 49). The left-over core carries the slowest factor, camera azimuth (0.94).
  • Moving along each block changes only its factor: the cube slides, recolours, the light shifts colour, brightens, or the camera moves back.
  • The three smallest factors in the image (gripper, arm pose, cube yaw) are not kept by this 25-minute autoencoder, and their rungs find no sharp cut.

The bend and the matrices in this experiment

The synthetic worlds use a hand-made bend: four fixed tables W1 to W4 whose size is the bend strength (section 1). This experiment has no such tables. The numbers come from a simulator and a trained autoencoder, so the bend is the rendering itself and the matrices are the autoencoder's weights and the whitening matrix. In order:

1. Factors to image (the bend). The 15 factor numbers f are drawn independently and uniformly in the ranges below (not Gaussian), and the simulator renders the scene (ManiSkill3 PickCube, 96 × 96 colour images). A pair for rung k copies the factors of groups k+1 to 9 and redraws groups 1 to k.

{{ROWS62}}
#FactorNumbersRangeHow it enters the sceneLinear R²Network R²

The last two columns read each factor back from the 48 numbers (held-out R², 9,000 probe images; the linear column is the one used to decide which factors the autoencoder kept). A negative value means the small network does worse than guessing the average.

The map from f to the image is nonlinear: perspective projection, shading and overlap of objects, with no stack of tanh layers and no strength setting. Its effect shows in the last two columns: the slow factors (tint, brightness, camera) are read almost linearly (0.95 to 0.99), while cube position and colour are read better by the small network (0.97 and 0.91) than by a linear map (0.76 and 0.76), and the gripper only by the network (0.64 against 0.11).

2. Image to 48 numbers (the trained matrices). A convolutional autoencoder, trained only to redraw its input (no labels). The encoder is four convolutions with 4 × 4 kernels, stride 2 and GELU (3 → 32 → 64 → 128 → 128 channels; 96 → 48 → 24 → 12 → 6 pixels), then one linear map Wenc from 128 · 6 · 6 = 4,608 numbers to 48: {{E62_PARAMS}} weights in the encoder ({{E62_PARAMS_ALL}} with the decoder). Trained on the 96,000 pair images with Adam (learning rate 10−3, batches of 128) for 25 minutes = {{E62_STEPS}} steps; the last logged reconstruction error is {{E62_LOSS}} (mean squared, on pixel values from 0 to 1).

3. Whitening matrix. The 48 numbers e are whitened exactly as in section 1, with the matrix computed from the 48,000 encoded images of the rung pairs:

r = Wwh (e − μ),   Wwh = C−1/2 = V diag(λ1−1/2, …, λ48−1/2) VT,   C = V diag(λ) VT

Wwh is a symmetric 48 × 48 matrix (stored as matrix in results/e62/blocks.pt). The spread of the 48 numbers runs from λ1 = {{E62_EVMAX}} to λ48 = {{E62_EVMIN}} (ratio {{E62_COND}}), so whitening multiplies the weakest direction by {{E62_SVMAX}} and the strongest by {{E62_SVMIN}}; no direction reached the floor of 10−5. Check: Wwh C Wwh equals the identity to 2.4 · 10−7. After this the pair differences are measured and split as in section 2.

4. Assumptions

  1. The matched pairs are given (copied from the generator; in 3.6 built by clustering, with the true number of clusters supplied).
  2. The layers are independent, each with unit spread, and have known sizes (4 numbers) and known order.
  3. The map from hidden numbers to points keeps all the information and is bent only moderately.
  4. Each layer's change lives in its own directions, which the bend increasingly blurs.
  5. 30,000 samples are used to whiten and 30,000 pairs per rung.
Scope. The results are on synthetic worlds generated for this study and one simulated-robot test.
