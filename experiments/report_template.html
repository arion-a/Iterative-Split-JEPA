How Iterative SplitJEPA works

One worked example, real numbers from the real code. Nothing here needs mathematics beyond "subtract two lists" and "find the long direction of a cloud".

The one-sentence idea A video frame becomes a dot in a space with many coordinates. The things that change in the video (pose, position, identity…) change at different speeds. The method looks at pairs of dots that are the same in the slow things and different in a fast thing, and finds which directions in the space are "the fast thing". It removes them, and repeats for the next layer.

1The data: layers of hidden numbers

Everything starts from hidden numbers that the data generator picks. The method never sees them; they are the answer key used only for scoring.

The example used on every page: 3 layers, each layer holds 2 numbers, so one sample is a list of 6 numbers.

"Three numbers" vs "four numbers" — why you saw both The real experiments use 5 layers of 4 numbers (20 numbers per sample). To draw something readable I used smaller examples (3 numbers, then this 6-number one). Same method, only smaller. From here on, only this 6-number example is used.

How "fast" and "slow" are set: I set it when I generate the data. At every time step each number becomes keep% × old value + fresh random part. Keep 20% means mostly fresh each frame (fast); keep 95% means almost unchanged (slow).

2From hidden numbers to a dot the method can see

The generator bends the 6 hidden numbers with a fixed function (this stands in for "a camera turning a scene into pixels"). An encoder then turns the result into a vector of 6 coordinates, called e1 to e6. That vector is the dot. In this example the encoder does nothing (untrained), so you see only the effect of the bend.

The bend mixes the layers: no single embedding coordinate equals one hidden number any more.

{{FIG_MIX}}

What this picture is: a 6 × 6 table of numbers. Each cell is how strongly one hidden number (row) and one embedding coordinate (column) move together, measured over 4,000 samples (0 = unrelated, 1 = move in lockstep). It is a summary computed once; it does not change over time. Darker off-diagonal cells are the mixing described below.

The exact rule behind it (the formula) The 6 numbers are held as two halves: left = (A1, A2, B1) and right = (B2, C1, C2). Four steps, each adds a bent copy of one half onto the other:
step 1right = right + tanh( left × W1 )
step 2left = left + tanh( right × W2 )
step 3right = right + tanh( left × W3 )
step 4left = left + tanh( right × W4 )
W1…W4 are fixed 3 × 3 tables of numbers (shown below), "×" is the usual table-times-list multiplication, and tanh squashes a number smoothly into the range −1 to 1. The output is the 6 coordinates e1…e6. These steps are what the code does (checked to reproduce it exactly).

Is the picture a matrix that changes with time steps? Two kinds of "matrix" appear here, and neither changes over time. (1) The weights W1…W4 are fixed for the whole experiment: the bend is the same at every time step. (2) The shaded picture is a table of correlations, computed once over many samples, so it is a fixed summary. What changes with time is only the hidden numbers themselves (the three curves in section 1); each new frame is a new list of 6 numbers pushed through the same four steps.

3Whitening, and what "covariance" means

Covariance, plainly A table that says how much each coordinate varies, and how much each pair of coordinates move together across all the dots. It is a summary of the cloud's shape: a stretched, tilted cloud has big numbers along the stretch; a perfectly round cloud has the same number everywhere and zeros elsewhere. I will call it the spread table.

Whitening uses that table to remove the stretching: it shrinks the long directions and grows the short ones until the cloud is round. After it, the spread table is just "1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere", so no direction is special merely because it was stretched.

{{FIG_WHITE}}

Left: the real spread table of the embedding (strong colours = coordinates moving together). Second: after whitening, it is plain. Right: two of the coordinates before and after. The bend itself is still there; whitening only evens out the stretch.

4Matched pairs: what they are and the basis of matching

A pair is two whole samples (two rows of 6 numbers). For the question "where is layer A?", the pair must agree on every slower layer (B and C) and be free to differ in A.

What the basis of matching is In these experiments, I know the hidden numbers because I wrote the generator, so I simply copy the ones that must agree. That is the whole basis. The splitter itself never sees the hidden numbers, only the two dots. For the robot images the pairs came from the simulator's scene settings. On ordinary video nobody gives you this, and the only automatic way I tested (grouping dots into clusters and pairing dots within a cluster) worked at rung 2 and faded by rungs 4–5, in my own synthetic worlds only.

Why rung 2's pair also redraws A: rung 1 already removes A's direction, so whatever A does in a rung-2 pair is invisible. The pair only has to agree on what is slower than B, that is C.

5Rung 1: subtract, measure the spread, read off the long axes

  1. Subtract. For each rung-1 pair, subtract the two whitened dots coordinate by coordinate: a list of 6 numbers (the "difference" row above). The coordinates of A change, the others cancel (up to the bend). Do this for 30,000 pairs.
  2. Spread table of the differences. This is where covariance is used: the same kind of table as in step 3, but computed on the differences instead of the dots. It shows which directions the pairs differ in.
  3. Find the long axes (eigendecomposition). A cloud of differences is an oval. "Eigendecomposition" is just the operation that reads off the oval's axes: the long ones and how long, the short ones and how short. Here: the sizes are {{EV1}} (two big, four much smaller).
  4. Cut. The 2 long axes = block of layer A (the "variant" block, V1). The 4 short axes = the directions that did not change between the pairs (the "invariant" content). Gap = smallest long ÷ largest short = {{GAP1}} : 1. A big gap = a clean cut; about 1 = no block there.
{{FIG_EIG1}}

Left: spread table of the differences (real). Middle: the differences along the two long axes (red) are wide; along the four short axes (blue) they spread far less. Right: the eight sizes.

6What is passed on to rung 2, and why C appears at the end

What carries to the next rung is the set of kept directions: the 4 invariant directions from rung 1. That set is exactly "the part of the embedding that did not change when A changed". Rung 2 only looks inside it.

{{FIG_FLOW}}
  • Rung 2 builds its pairs (agree on C, differ in B), subtracts the dots, but now keeps only the 4 kept directions, and computes the spread table of the differences there.
  • It has 2 long axes (B) and 2 much smaller ones. Sizes: {{EV2}}, gap {{GAP2}} : 1.
  • Rung 2 does not recover C. It recovers B. C is what is left over after removing A and B: the 2 directions that did not change in any pair. This answers your question: each step keeps the content that stayed the same, so the last thing standing is the most persistent layer.

How this relates to the SplitJEPA paper The paper trains the encoder so that a fixed slice of its output stays the same across matched pairs. Here the encoder is only used as a frozen map and the split is found afterwards with the steps above. I did not run the paper's trained split side by side with this, so there is no claim that this is better, only that it works under the conditions shown.

9Check it by hand: a case small enough to do on paper

Everything above is computed by code. Here is the whole splitting done with pencil on the smallest possible case, and the real code run on the same numbers. If the method is fundamental, both must agree, and they do.

The tiny world 3 hidden numbers (a fast, b middle, c slow), one per layer. The embedding is r = (r1, r2, r3) with r1 = (a + b)/√2   r2 = (a − b)/√2   r3 = c. This is a pure mixing of a and b (a turn by 45°). It is already "whitened": the three directions are perpendicular and equally scaled, so no whitening step is needed.

Rung 1: pairs agree on b and c, differ in a

Take 4 pairs whose a-values differ by δa = +2, −2, +1, −1 (b and c identical). The embedding difference of a pair is D = (δa/√2, δa/√2, 0). So the four D's are (1.414, 1.414, 0), (−1.414, −1.414, 0), (0.707, 0.707, 0), (−0.707, −0.707, 0).

Spread table (average of D times D, entry by entry): the average of δa² is (4 + 4 + 1 + 1)/4 = 2.5, and each entry is 2.5 × (1/√2)(1/√2) = 1.25 where the pair of coordinates is (1,1), (1,2), (2,1), (2,2), and 0 elsewhere:

Long axes: this table is 2.5 × (a direction u u-transposed) with u = (1, 1, 0)/√2. Such a table has one non-zero size, 2.5, along u, and two zeros. So the sizes are 2.5, 0, 0, and the block for layer a is the direction (0.707, 0.707, 0). Gap = 2.5 ÷ 0 (infinite: the cut is perfect because nothing is noisy). The kept directions are everything perpendicular to u: (0.707, −0.707, 0) and (0, 0, 1).

Rung 2: pairs agree on c, differ in a and b

Take pairs with (δa, δb) = (1, 3), (−2, −3), (3, 1), (0.5, −1). Look only along the two kept directions:

  • along (0.707, −0.707, 0): the difference is (D1 − D2)/√2 = ((δa + δb) − (δa − δb))/2 = δb. (δa cancels, which is why "A is hidden by rung 1".)
  • along (0, 0, 1): the difference is δc = 0.

The spread table inside the kept directions is [[mean δb², 0], [0, 0]] with mean δb² = (9 + 9 + 1 + 1)/4 = 5: sizes 5, 0. The block for layer b is the first kept direction: (0.707, −0.707, 0). What is left is (0, 0, 1) = c, the slowest layer, untouched by every pair.

What this does and does not verify It verifies the algebra: subtract pairs, spread table, long axes, remove, repeat gives exactly the layers in the exact, noise-free, perfectly mixed case. It does not verify the experimental claims (bent maps, noisy pairs, trained encoders, images): those cannot be done by hand and are numerical results, with the limits listed in section 8.
